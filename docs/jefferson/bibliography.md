# Bibliography — Jefferson transcription

## 1. Primary literature (Gail Jefferson)

The definitive archive of Jefferson's publications, most as free PDFs, is the
[LISO archive at UC Santa Barbara](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/).

### The canonical glossary

- **Jefferson, G. (2004). "Glossary of transcript symbols with an introduction."**
  In G. H. Lerner (Ed.), *Conversation Analysis: Studies from the first generation*
  (pp. 13–31). Amsterdam/Philadelphia: John Benjamins.
  [PDF (scan, no text layer)](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Transcript.pdf) ·
  [publisher](https://benjamins.com/catalog/pbns.125.02jef)
  — **The** authoritative symbol list, written by Jefferson herself late in life,
  with commentary on why each convention exists. This is the document our grammar
  should treat as canon. Note: it is a narrated glossary, not a formal grammar.

### Jefferson on transcription itself (methodology papers)

- **Jefferson, G. (1983). "Issues in the transcription of naturally-occurring talk:
  Caricature versus capturing pronunciational particulars."** *Tilburg Papers in
  Language and Literature*, No. 34.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Caricature.pdf)
  — Her defense of non-standard orthography ("wuz", "yer") against the charge that
  it caricatures speakers; explains the philosophy behind writing what was said,
  not what "should" have been said.
- **Jefferson, G. (1985). "An exercise in the transcription and analysis of laughter."**
  In T. van Dijk (Ed.), *Handbook of Discourse Analysis*, Vol. 3 (pp. 25–34).
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Laughter2.pdf)
  — Why laughter is transcribed as particles (`huh/hah/heh`, `(h)` in words) rather
  than glossed as `((laughs))`; the classic argument that detail pays off analytically.
- **Jefferson, G. (1996). "A case of transcriptional stereotyping."**
  *Journal of Pragmatics, 26*(2), 159–170.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Transcriptional_Stereotyping.pdf)
  — Self-critical study of how transcriber expectations bias what gets written down.
- **Jefferson, G. (1988/1983). "Notes on a possible metric which provides for a
  'standard maximum' silence of approximately one second in conversation."**
  In Roger & Bull (Eds.), *Conversation* (pp. 166–196). Clevedon: Multilingual Matters.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/standard_maximum_silence.pdf)
  — The empirical basis for timing silences in tenths of a second.

### Foundational papers where the notation first appears at scale

- **Sacks, H., Schegloff, E. A., & Jefferson, G. (1974). "A simplest systematics for
  the organization of turn-taking for conversation."** *Language, 50*(4), 696–735.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Systematics.pdf)
  — Contains one of the first full published descriptions of the transcription system
  (appendix) and states its design goal: "to get as much of the actual sound as
  possible into [the] transcripts, while still making them accessible to
  linguistically unsophisticated readers" (p. 734).
- **Jefferson, G. (1973). "A case of precision timing in ordinary conversation."**
  *Semiotica, 9*(1), 47–96.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Precision.pdf)
  — Early showcase of overlap notation and why bracket placement precision matters.
- **Jefferson, G. (2004 [1975]). "A sketch of some orderly aspects of overlap in
  natural conversation."** In Lerner (Ed.), pp. 43–59.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/sketch.pdf)
  — Overlap notation in analytic use.
- **Schegloff, E. A., Jefferson, G., & Sacks, H. (1977). "The preference for
  self-correction in the organization of repair in conversation."** *Language, 53*, 361–382.
  [PDF](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/Repair.pdf)
  — Cut-off (`-`) notation in analytic use.

## 2. Secondary literature: descriptions of the system

- **Hepburn, A., & Bolden, G. B. (2013). "The conversation analytic approach to
  transcription."** In Sidnell & Stivers (Eds.), *The Handbook of Conversation
  Analysis* (pp. 57–76). Wiley-Blackwell.
  [Author PDF](https://gbolden.comminfo.rutgers.edu/RussianCA/publications/Hepburn_Bolden_Transcription.pdf)
  — The best modern systematic description. Organizes conventions into: layout;
  temporal/sequential relations; speech delivery; metacommentary/uncertain hearings;
  non-speech activity. Documents variants (`^` for `↑`, `¿`, `?,`) and responds to
  critiques. We relied on this heavily.
- **Hepburn, A., & Bolden, G. B. (2017). *Transcribing for Social Research*.** London: Sage.
  — Book-length expansion of the above; the most comprehensive current guide
  (recommended as such by the DOTE project). Not open access.
- **Atkinson, J. M., & Heritage, J. (Eds.) (1984). *Structures of Social Action:
  Studies in Conversation Analysis*.** Cambridge University Press.
  — Its "Transcript notation" appendix (pp. ix–xvi) was for decades the standard
  citation for the conventions and is the source many course handouts reproduce.
- **Hutchby, I., & Wooffitt, R. (2008). *Conversation Analysis* (2nd ed.).** Polity.
  — Widely used textbook; its glossary (reproduced e.g. in this
  [PDX handout](https://web.pdx.edu/~jkh/Common%20Transcription%20Conventions%20in%20Conversation%20Analysis.pdf))
  adds symbols like `!` (animated tone) and `*` (croaky voice) — note the variance
  from Hepburn & Bolden's `#` for creak.
- **Hepburn, A. (2004). "Crying: Notes on description, transcription, and interaction."**
  *Research on Language and Social Interaction, 37*, 251–290.
  — Source of the newer upset-related symbols: `~` tremulous voice, `.snih` sniffs,
  extensions of `°°...°°` whispering.
- **ten Have, P. (2007). *Doing Conversation Analysis* (2nd ed.).** Sage.
  — Practical guide including how to type transcripts in word processors.

## 3. Critiques, comparisons, and machine-readability discourse

- **Ochs, E. (1979). "Transcription as theory."** In Ochs & Schieffelin (Eds.),
  *Developmental Pragmatics* (pp. 43–72). Academic Press.
  — Classic argument that every transcription system encodes theoretical assumptions.
- **Bucholtz, M. (2000). "The politics of transcription."** *Journal of Pragmatics,
  32*, 1439–1465. And **Bucholtz, M. (2007). "Variation in transcription."**
  *Discourse Studies, 9*, 784–808.
  — Documents how the "same" conventions are applied inconsistently across
  researchers — directly relevant to our ambiguities list.
- **Mondada, L. (2007). "Commentary: transcript variations and the indexicality of
  transcribing practices."** *Discourse Studies, 9*, 809–821.
- **O'Connell, D. C., & Kowal, S. (1994). "Some current transcription systems for
  spoken discourse: a critical analysis."** *Pragmatics, 4*, 81–107.
  — Systematic critique of CA notation's internal inconsistencies from outside CA.
- **Couper-Kuhlen, E., & Barth-Weingarten, D. (2011). "A system for transcribing
  talk-in-interaction: GAT 2."** *Gesprächsforschung, 12*, 1–51.
  — The German competitor system, designed *because* Jefferson notation was felt to
  be under-specified for prosody; instructive contrast.
- **MacWhinney, B., & Wagner, J. (2010). "Transcribing, searching and data sharing:
  The CLAN software and the TalkBank data repository."** *Gesprächsforschung, 11*, 154–173.
  — Describes CHAT-CA: the TalkBank project's machine-readable re-encoding of
  Jefferson notation (dedicated Unicode characters, strict word-level rules).
  The most serious prior attempt to make Jefferson parseable.
- **Umair, M., Mertens, J. B., Albert, S., & de Ruiter, J. P. (2022). "GailBot: An
  automatic transcription system for Conversation Analysis."** *Dialogue & Discourse,
  13*(1), 63–95. [DOI 10.5210/dad.2022.103](https://doi.org/10.5210/dad.2022.103) ·
  [open access](https://journals.uic.edu/ojs/index.php/dad/article/view/11392)
  — Documents which Jefferson features are algorithmically decidable (pauses, overlap,
  laughter, speech rate) and which are not; useful implicit formalization.
- **Albert, S. "Pandoc + Markdown for Conversation Analytic Transcripts"** (blog, 2015).
  [Post](https://saulalbert.net/blog/pandoc-markdown-for-ca-papers-ii/)
  — Practitioner discourse on plain-text/Unicode problems: combining underline,
  U+2308 ceiling brackets for overlap, bullet-operator inbreaths, the CAfont.
- **DOTE (Aalborg University): "Jeffersonian style CA transcription conventions
  supported by DOTE."** [Help page](https://bigsoftvideo.github.io/DOTE/jefferson.html)
  — A shipping editor's formal model of a Jefferson transcript ("neighbourhoods",
  primary/secondary speaker lines) and an explicit normative/agnostic/clash taxonomy
  for legacy symbols. Closest thing to prior art for our extension.
- **EMCA wiki, "Transcription Resources."** [Page](https://emcawiki.net/Transcription_Resources)
  — Living index of tools, guides, and the transcription literature.

## 4. Sources of high-quality published transcripts (test-corpus material)

- **Jefferson's own papers at the [LISO archive](https://liso-archives.liso.ucsb.edu/Jefferson/)**
  — every paper is dense with transcripts by the inventor herself; the 1985 laughter
  paper and the 2004 glossary contain particularly symbol-rich extracts.
  (Scanned PDFs; transcripts must be re-keyed by hand, which is itself good dogfooding.)
- **CABank / TalkBank** (https://ca.talkbank.org/) — includes Jefferson's
  Newport Beach corpus and her Watergate/Nixon-tapes transcripts in CHAT-CA format;
  CC-licensed. Good for volume, but note it uses TalkBank's Unicode re-encoding,
  not typewriter-style Jefferson.
- **Atkinson & Heritage (1984)** — book-length collection of first-generation CA
  studies, all with high-quality transcripts (e.g. Pomerantz on assessments,
  Jefferson on troubles-talk laughter).
- **Hepburn & Bolden (2013)** chapter excerpts — short, verified, feature-dense
  examples (NSPCC helpline, Holt corpus, "Location, Location, Location" laughter
  extract with nested overlap + latching + pitch arrows). Good adversarial test cases.
- **Schegloff's Transcription Module** (UCLA,
  https://www.sscnet.ucla.edu/soc/faculty/schegloff/TranscriptionProject/) —
  training materials with audio + reference transcripts.
- **Whalen & Zimmerman (1998). "Observations on the expression and management of
  emotion in naturally occurring activities: the case of 'hysteria' in calls to 911."**
  *Social Psychology Quarterly, 61*, 141–159. — extreme aspiration/upset notation.

**Licensing note:** published transcript excerpts are quotable in small amounts for
testing/documentation with attribution, but we should not commit large verbatim
corpora; prefer CABank's licensed materials or synthetic samples modeled on the
published ones (RESEARCH.md §5 already flags this).
